Eighty months of production runtime after my last commit on it. Four systems, four handovers, and nobody had to call me back. The fourth was switched off deliberately when the product it served was retired — which is the other half of doing this properly.

“The parts nobody photographs — error handling, migration paths, the second week after launch — decide whether software is trustworthy at 3 a.m.”

Six years of shipping software other people depend on: scheduling systems, internal tools, the unglamorous layer underneath a product. I have owned things end to end and handed things over cleanly, which is a different and much more underrated skill.

Writing the handover doc is the part everyone skips. It is also the only reason those four lines above keep going without me — so on this site it gets a chart and the demos get a card.

This page exists because a bullet list never conveyed any of that. So I put the whole thing somewhere you can walk through — then made very sure you could skip the walk.

Design note · the synthesis, and why it is not a feature mashup

  • ◈The choice section is a metro map, because that is what it actually is. A fork that rejoins. Prose said it; now the geometry says it — two lines with their own time scales, their own stations, and one shared terminus drawn as a real interchange bracket. Everyone on earth can read a transit diagram without instructions, which is exactly why almost nobody uses one on a portfolio.
  • ◈The dial is the argument, not a toy. Drag “how long have you actually got” and watch what happens: the gold line reaches its terminus at 90 seconds — before the cyan line has left its first station. The page stops claiming the Codex is enough and lets you watch it be enough. The recommended departure re-flags itself as you drag. That is a rhetorical device rendered as an instrument.
  • ◈The call to action is a departures board. Destination · duration · gate · status, in monospace, with a live clock and split-flap names that shuffle into place on reveal. It reframes three buttons as three services, all terminating at the same archive — which is the one thing this section has to make unmistakable.
  • ◈The About section is a service record, because the claim above it is a claim about time. “Things that keep working after I leave the room” cannot be proved by a skill bar, so it is drawn instead: one lifeline per system, with the ship date, a hard marker on the day I handed it over, and the run that continues past that marker to today. All four rows share one coordinate space, so a tick at 57.3% is 2023 on every line — the chart is readable as a chart, not as an ornament.
  • ◈The animation carries the argument's word order. Each lifeline plays three beats: the solid segment builds, the handover marker snaps on, and only then the dashed segment runs out to the present. The last thing you watch is the part that happens without me — which is the entire point of the section. It costs nothing per frame: three CSS transitions keyed off the same reveal class the rest of the page already uses. One row ends in ✕ rather than an arrow, because a system switched off on purpose is not a failure and pretending otherwise would make the other three less believable.
  • ◈Skill percentages are gone, and calibration replaced them. Nobody can act on “React 92%.” Three tiers can: put me on it alone, fluent but not the expert, hire someone better and I'll support them. The third tier is the one that makes the first two credible. Their shapes fall in confidence with their contents — the top tier is filled and lit, the middle outlined, the last dashed and unfilled.
  • ◈No two cards are the same shape, and no two read in the same order. The three principles: A is number-led (ghost numeral, icon beside a label, then the claim), B is band-led (a colour rail carries the icon and a vertical label; the text sits beside it), C is title-led (the claim comes first and the cube signs off at the bottom). The work section drops the triplet grid entirely for a bento: a 7-column landscape card, a 5-column tall card whose scene lands under the text, and a full-width horizontal slab. Corner radii rotate around every row so a grid never reads as a stamp.
  • ◈Variety with a rule, not variety for its own sake. Each card's shape follows its content: the card with three metrics gets the two-column body, the card with the shortest copy gets the rail, the card with the longest gets the width. Randomised layouts read as noise; layouts derived from their content read as authored.
  • ◈One idea, not three. Aurora gave atmosphere, isometric gave dimension, the exploded boot gave ritual — but all three were secretly about a world being assembled. So the page has exactly one hero concept: a broken world rebuilt in front of you, in 3D, inside living air. Three hero moments competing is how merges fail.
  • ◈The city is the data. Every block's height is proportional to a real number — 6 years, 10 projects, 19 places, 16 puzzles, 100 memories, 5 endings. Hover any block and the readout below tells you what it is. A bar chart disguised as a toy, so the decoration earns its place instead of just occupying it.
  • ◈One light source, obeyed everywhere. Upper-left. Every block's top face is brightness(1.28), its left face .82, its right face .58 — never randomised. Every glass panel catches the same light on its top edge, weighted left, and down its left border. This single rule is most of why it reads as real rather than decorated.
  • ◈A motion budget, spent once per screen height. Boot assembles · hero tilts · cards lift · bars fill · CTA breathes. Never two hero motions at the same time. Restraint is the reason it feels expensive instead of busy.
  • ◈The cyclist was re-cast, not pasted in. He now rides on the isometric plate, rim-lit in the world's cyan — which makes him a scale figure, the classic architectural-rendering trick for making a model read as a place. Same joy, but he now serves the composition. Click him. Press B to send him home; the choice is remembered.
  • ◈The air is alive but cheap. The aurora is a half-resolution canvas of six additive blobs under a 74 px blur — a few hundred bytes of state, one draw per frame, and it pauses the moment the tab is hidden. A cursor-tracked light layer makes the space feel physical for the cost of one transform.
  • ◈Replace before shipping: the portrait, the quote and bio paragraphs, the email and social handles, the four systems in the service record — their ship dates, handover dates and month counts drive the chart geometry directly, so edit the --s / --h / --e percentages on each row when you swap them — the three calibration tiers, and all three project cards. The shapes are correct; the facts are invented.

Screen review · pre-production

Five screens, before a line of code

The first five surfaces a visitor meets, rendered at the real palette, the real layout, and the real copy from the design document. Three of them decide whether this project works: screen 2 is the only moment a visitor is asked to learn something new, screen 4 is where a recruiter's ninety seconds are actually spent, and screen 5 is where the world offers them the exit.

1

Three options, not eight. Hick's Law is load-bearing here — the first screen of an unfamiliar experience is where choice paralysis is most expensive. Each option states its real cost in time so nobody enters by accident.

2

The skip is styled up, not down. It sits in the middle position, in a cooler tint than the muted third option — visible without competing. Making it look like a failure state would defeat the entire two-layer architecture.

3

One saturated colour in the frame. Cold cyan bleeding from behind the gate is the only chroma on screen. It is --luma #7FD4E8, so the first colour a visitor sees is the Guardian's, before they know what a Guardian is.

4

Left-anchored, not centred. Centred menus are the default and read as a template. Anchoring the block to the left third leaves the gate's negative space intact and lets the composition breathe asymmetrically.

5

Arabic present at the threshold. The locale toggle is on screen one, not buried in settings — RTL is a first-class locale in this build, and the title screen is where that promise is made.

1

Attention is the mechanic. Nothing is clicked. The glyph responds to proximity, so the first thing the world teaches is that looking at something is an action — which is the grammar every later puzzle uses.

2

The counter is the only UI. 0 / 7 in hairline mono. It appears after the first glyph lights, so the visitor discovers the goal by achieving part of it rather than by being briefed.

3

The narrator sets type in italic serif. His lines are the only italic in the entire product — a permanent signal that this voice is a recording, not the interface speaking.

4

The one place novelty is affordable. An unfamiliar interaction in minute one is an invitation; the same interaction in minute forty is friction. Everything after this screen uses conventional bindings.

5

No time pressure, no failure. A visitor can sit here for six minutes. Holding still for thirty seconds is itself a hidden achievement — the design rewards patience rather than punishing it.

One float drives everything. uRestoration sets the water level, the jet, the lit-window count, the fog, the saturation LUT, and the music stems. Progress costs one uniform write per change — no scene rebuild, no per-frame cost.

The single most important interaction. Fires at 75 seconds with zero memories, or on exit intent. No modal, no dark overlay, no “are you sure?” — a quiet bar with a real Open and a real way to stay.

Four decisions I need before Phase 0

Everything above is reversible cheaply right now and expensive later. These four are the ones that change what gets built in week one.

Q1

Is the skip option positioned correctly?

It currently sits second, styled cooler than the primary but warmer than the tertiary. The alternative is a smaller link below the three options — less prominent, less honest. My recommendation: keep it second. It is the difference between a portfolio and a trap.

Q2

Third-person camera, or first-person?

Every screen here assumes third-person: the visitor sees a character, which makes the world's scale legible and the Islands dramatic. First-person is cheaper — no character model, no animation set — but flattens every vertical moment. My recommendation: third-person, with a simple silhouetted figure rather than a detailed one.

Q3

Does the Codex read as part of the world, or as an escape from it?

Screen 4 commits to parchment, the same surface as the memory cards, with the same sigils and category colours. If it should instead read as a clean modern document — deliberately outside the fiction — that is a legitimate different answer and it changes the type and palette work in Phase 1.

Q4

Is “what broke” going in?

A required, build-enforced field on every project naming one real failure. It is the most credibility-bearing and the most personally uncomfortable decision in the document. My recommendation: yes. It is what separates this from a portfolio where nothing ever went wrong.
